# Obstructive Sleep Apnea — clinical reference summary

*Synthetic educational summary in the style of public-domain NIH/MedlinePlus topic pages, written for the UW Lens demo corpus. Not medical advice and not an official NIH publication.*

## What it is
In obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) the upper airway repeatedly narrows or closes during sleep, causing pauses in breathing (apneas), shallow breathing (hypopneas), loud snoring, gasping, and drops in blood oxygen. People often feel very sleepy during the day.

## Diagnosis
A sleep study (polysomnography in a sleep lab, or a home sleep apnea test) counts breathing events per hour of sleep — the apnea-hypopnea index (AHI):
- Mild: AHI 5 to 14.
- Moderate: AHI 15 to 29.
- Severe: AHI 30 or more.
The lowest oxygen saturation during sleep is also reported.

## Treatment
Continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) keeps the airway open with a mask worn during sleep. Treatment adherence is often defined as using CPAP at least 4 hours a night on 70% of nights; modern devices record usage data. Other options are oral appliances, weight loss, positional therapy and, for some people, surgery or hypoglossal nerve stimulation.

## Why it matters
Untreated moderate or severe sleep apnea is linked to high blood pressure, atrial fibrillation, heart failure, stroke, type 2 diabetes and motor vehicle crashes from daytime sleepiness. Good CPAP use lowers sleepiness and improves blood pressure.
