# Type 2 Diabetes — clinical reference summary

*Synthetic educational summary in the style of public-domain NIH/MedlinePlus topic pages, written for the UW Lens demo corpus. Not medical advice and not an official NIH publication.*

## What it is
Type 2 diabetes is a chronic condition in which the body does not use insulin well (insulin resistance) and, over time, the pancreas cannot make enough insulin to keep blood glucose in a healthy range. It is the most common form of diabetes in adults.

## Diagnosis
Diabetes is usually diagnosed with one of the following, confirmed by repeat testing:
- Hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) of 6.5% or higher.
- Fasting plasma glucose of 126 mg/dL (7.0 mmol/L) or higher after at least 8 hours without food.
- Two-hour plasma glucose of 200 mg/dL or higher during an oral glucose tolerance test.
- A random plasma glucose of 200 mg/dL or higher with classic symptoms of high blood sugar.

An HbA1c between 5.7% and 6.4% indicates prediabetes.

## Treatment
Management starts with nutrition, physical activity and weight loss. Metformin is commonly the first medicine. Other classes include SGLT2 inhibitors and GLP-1 receptor agonists, which also lower cardiovascular and kidney risk in some people, and insulin when other treatments are not enough. Many adults aim for an HbA1c below 7%, with individualized targets.

## Complications to ask about
Long-standing or poorly controlled diabetes damages blood vessels and nerves. Key complications are diabetic retinopathy (eyes), diabetic kidney disease with protein in the urine (albuminuria) and falling eGFR, peripheral neuropathy and foot ulcers, heart attack, and stroke. Annual eye exams, urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR), eGFR and foot checks are recommended.

## Prognosis factors
Outcomes depend on age at onset, duration, glucose control over time (HbA1c trend), blood pressure, lipids, smoking, and whether complications are present. Early onset with sustained poor control carries the highest long-term risk.
